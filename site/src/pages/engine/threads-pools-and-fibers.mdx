[Jobs and task graphs](/engine/jobs-and-task-graphs/) decided *what* runs and in what order. This lesson is about what it runs *on*, and about one awkward moment: a job that has started, handed out work to other jobs, and now needs their results before it can finish. What its core does in the meantime is the difference between threads, thread pools, fibers and coroutines.

## Threads, briefly

An OS thread is a stack plus saved registers, and it's **preemptive**: the OS can stop it at any instruction, and parks it when it waits on a lock or event. That suits unrelated programs sharing a machine. For thousands of small jobs in one frame, it costs too much:

| | Roughly |
|---|---|
| Creating and starting a thread | tens of microseconds |
| A context switch between threads | a few microseconds, plus refilling the caches afterwards |
| A thread's stack | 1 MiB reserved by default on Windows, 8 MiB on Linux |
| A typical game job | 10 to 100 microseconds |

So engines create their threads once, at startup.

## Named threads

Some threads have a fixed job and a name. In Unreal, a **game thread** runs gameplay, a **rendering thread** turns the game's view of the world into draw calls, and on APIs that support it an **RHI thread** only submits work to the driver. Everything else runs on **worker threads**. Why dedicate a thread at all:

- **Some APIs belong to one thread.** Windowing and input usually stay on the main thread; older graphics APIs expect one thread to own the device context.
- **Pipelining.** Frame *N + 1*'s gameplay runs while frame *N* is being rendered, and frame *N − 1* is on the GPU.
- **State ownership.** The render thread keeps its own copy of what it needs (Unreal's render proxies), so the game thread can change the world without locks. They talk only through a queue of render commands.

<EngineWidget kind="pipeline" />

With the defaults (10 ms of gameplay, 8 ms of rendering, 9 ms on the GPU):

| Threads | Between frames | Frame rate | Update to pixels |
|---|---|---|---|
| One thread does it all | 18 ms | 56 fps | 27 ms |
| Game + render thread | 10 ms | 100 fps | 27 ms |
| Game + render + RHI | 10 ms | 100 fps | 27 ms |

The frame rate is set by the slowest stage instead of the sum of two. The cost is **frames in flight**: almost three frames are in progress at once, so update-to-pixels doesn't improve, and when one stage falls behind the others queue behind it. Engines bound that: Unreal's game thread waits at the end of its tick so it stays at most about a frame ahead of the render thread, which waits for the GPU in turn. Try **GPU 12 ms**: the frame rate drops to the GPU's, and the time from update to pixels stretches to about 40 ms. The RHI split only pays when rendering itself is the bottleneck: try **Game 6, Render 12**.

A named thread is still one thread. Its movement, animation, AI, visibility and command building are spread across the worker pool as jobs. Named threads give you ownership and pipelining; the pool gives you the cores.

## Worker pools

The workers are a **thread pool**: one thread per core (often minus the named threads' cores), fed from the job queues, and often pinned so caches stay warm. Naughty Dog's engine on PS4 ran six workers, each locked to a core.

An idle worker can **spin** on the queue (reacts in nanoseconds, burns power and steals time from its hyperthread sibling), **yield** its time slice, or **sleep** on a condition variable or semaphore (free, but microseconds to wake). Most pools spin briefly and then sleep.

## When a job waits

A party's update spawns a job per unit and needs all their results before it can finish. What does its worker do meanwhile? The widget runs one frame: six party updates that each fan out into six unit jobs (with **Waits inside waits**, two of those units run a path search that fans out again), then two dozen unrelated jobs. Hover any block to see what it is.

<EngineWidget kind="waiting" />

**Block.** The worker sleeps until the children are done, which is what `future.get()` or a join inside a job does. The core sits idle (the hatched blocks), and if waiting jobs outnumber the workers, nobody is left to run their children: with **4 cores**, the first four parties block every worker before a single unit job starts. A **deadlock**, from a perfectly reasonable piece of code.

**Help while waiting.** The waiting worker runs other jobs from the queue *on top of* the waiting job, on the same stack, until the children are done. No core idles and nothing deadlocks. The cost is in the nesting: the waiting job can only continue once the job on top of it returns, so if it picked up a 60 µs job just before its children finished, it waits another 60 µs for nothing. Each nested job also adds stack frames: with **Waits inside waits**, workers end up five jobs deep.

**Fibers.** A fiber is a stack and registers, like a thread, but nobody preempts it. Every job runs on one. When it waits, the worker parks it and switches to a fresh fiber; when the children are done, the next free worker switches back to it, call stack intact. A switch saves a few registers: no kernel.

**Coroutines.** The same scheduling with C++20 coroutines: no stack, just a frame holding the job's locals, suspended by `co_await`. Cheaper to switch than a fiber, and a suspended job costs only its frame.

**A thread per job.** For comparison, no pool at all: every job is an OS thread, and waiting is the OS parking it.

With 8 cores and one level of waiting (6 waiting parties, 1,819 µs of work, a perfect split is 227 µs):

| When a job waits | Frame | Efficiency | Overhead | Worst late resume | Extra stack memory |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Block | 714 µs | 32% | 8 µs | none (3,748 µs blocked) | none |
| Help while waiting | 248 µs | 92% | 8 µs | 13 µs | none |
| Fibers | 248 µs | 92% | 23 µs | none | 14 fibers, 0.9 MiB |
| Coroutines | 246 µs | 92% | 12 µs | none | 14 frames, 14 KiB |
| A thread per job | 657 µs | 35% | 888 µs | none | 27 threads, 27 MiB |

With **Waits inside waits** (18 jobs waiting at some point), blocking deadlocks even with 16 cores, and helping reaches five jobs deep and keeps finished waits waiting for up to 49 µs on 8 cores and 99 µs on 4. Fibers and coroutines don't change shape at all.

Blocking is out: it wastes cores, and it deadlocks once enough jobs wait, a number nothing in the code tells you. A thread per job loses on overhead alone, and the thread spawning the jobs becomes the bottleneck. Helping, fibers and coroutines all keep every core busy and finish together; they differ in late resumes, stack depth, the memory parked work holds, and what the code may do.

```cpp
// Helping: wait by running other jobs until the counter reaches zero.
void wait_for(std::atomic<int>& counter) {
    while (counter.load(std::memory_order_acquire) > 0) {
        if (Job* j = queue.try_pop()) j->run();   // nests on this stack
        else std::this_thread::yield();
    }
}
```

<Tip kind="warn" title="Helping has a trap">
A helper can pick up a job that itself waits on something only the original waiting job can provide, and then neither finishes. Many systems restrict helping to jobs in the same group, or to the children being waited on, which removes most cycles at the cost of some idle time.
</Tip>

## Fibers in practice

Christian Gyrling's GDC 2015 talk, [*Parallelizing the Naughty Dog Engine Using Fibers*](https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1022186/Parallelizing-the-Naughty-Dog-Engine), is the reference design. Six workers, one per core, and 160 fibers: 128 with 64 KiB stacks and 32 with 512 KiB. The only way to wait is `WaitForCounter`: the fiber goes on the counter's wait list, and the worker takes a fresh fiber from the pool. *The Last of Us Remastered* ran 800 to 1,000 jobs a frame this way.

The same talk lists what fibers break:

- **Thread-local storage.** A fiber can resume on a different worker, and a compiler that cached a thread-local's address across the wait reads another thread's copy. Naughty Dog moved all thread-local access into a file the optimiser couldn't see through.
- **OS locks.** A mutex must be unlocked on the thread that locked it, and waiting on an OS primitive blocks the whole worker. Fiber code uses spin locks for short sections and job-aware mutexes, which park the fiber, for long ones.
- **Stack size.** Every fiber needs a stack for its deepest call, times the number of fibers. Hence the two pools.
- **Tools.** Debuggers and profilers assume a call stack belongs to a thread; a parked or thread-hopping fiber confuses them.

## Coroutines

```cpp
Task update_party(Party& p) {
    plan_moves(p);
    Counter done{(int)p.units.size()};
    for (Unit& u : p.units) spawn([&] { update_unit(u); }, done);
    co_await done;               // suspend; a worker resumes us when the units are done
    apply_results(p);
}
```

A suspended coroutine holds only the locals live across the suspension, often tens or hundreds of bytes, so a hundred thousand waiting jobs are affordable. Every pause is a visible `co_await`, so thread-local storage and locks are safe everywhere else. The price is **colouring**: only a coroutine can suspend, so if the wait is three calls deep, every function on the way must become a coroutine too. Fibers suspend from anywhere, which makes them easier to retrofit.

<Tip kind="note" title="What the compiler does, and what it leaves to you">
A function containing `co_await` is compiled into a state machine. Its locals live in a heap-allocated frame (which the compiler can sometimes place in the caller's frame instead), and `co_await` saves the resume point and returns to whoever resumed it. The language supplies only that mechanism: the task type, the awaitable counter and the hand-off to the worker pool are yours to write, or to take from a library.
</Tip>

| | Preemptive threads | Fibers | Stackless coroutines |
|---|---|---|---|
| Who decides when to switch | the OS, at any instruction | the job, at a wait | the job, at a `co_await` |
| Switch cost | microseconds, through the kernel | a few registers | cheaper still |
| Memory per waiting job | a full stack (MiB) | a fiber stack (KiB) | the frame (bytes) |
| Can wait from deep in a call | yes | yes | only if every caller is a coroutine |
| Thread-local storage and OS locks | work | break across a switch | safe outside suspension points |
| Debuggers and profilers | fully supported | partly | improving |

<Tip title="Why cooperative wins here">
Preemption exists so that code that doesn't cooperate still gets a fair share. In a job system every job is yours, short and written to finish, so yielding at known points costs nothing. Fibers and coroutines don't run work faster; they make waiting almost free.
</Tip>

## Designing so nothing waits

The cheapest wait is the one that doesn't happen. Split the job: *spawn* the units' jobs, and make *apply the results* a separate job that depends on them. That's the [task graph](/engine/jobs-and-task-graphs/) again, and nothing is ever half-finished. Fibers and coroutines earn their place where splitting is awkward: a long chain of steps with waits between, or a wait deep in a call stack.

flecs takes the splitting route: a system runs to the end, and its queued commands are merged at the next sync point. `set_threads` gives flecs its own worker pool for multi-threaded systems, and `set_task_threads` hands the same work to tasks on your job system instead.

## Takeaways

- **OS threads are preemptive and expensive** to create and switch. Create them once.
- **Named threads** own APIs and state and pipeline frames; the parallel work goes to a **worker pool**, one thread per core.
- **Blocking a worker** wastes a core and deadlocks once enough jobs wait. **Helping** keeps cores busy, at the cost of nested stacks and late resumes.
- **Fibers** park a waiting job with its whole stack; they break thread-local storage, OS locks and tools. **Coroutines** are tiny and explicit, but every function that waits has to be one.
- **Better still, don't wait in the middle**: split the job, and let the task graph run the second half.

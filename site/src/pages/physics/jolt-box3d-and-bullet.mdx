The deep dive behind [choosing an engine](/physics/choosing-an-engine/): you don't need it to choose, but it's where the differences show. Everything in the concept lessons, done for real, in 3D, three times over. **Jolt**, **Box3D** and **Bullet** are the three open-source rigid-body engines most worth knowing: one young, one at the height of its use in games, one that shaped a decade of engines and tools. All three run below on the same scenes, compiled to WebAssembly.

## Where they come from

**Jolt** is Jorrit Rouwe's engine (MIT licence). Its README lists *Horizon Forbidden West* and *Death Stranding 2: On the Beach* among the games using it. It's mature (version 5.6 at the time of writing) and broad: vehicles, soft bodies, ragdolls, characters, double precision for large worlds. Godot made it the default 3D physics engine for new projects in 4.6.

**Box3D** is Erin Catto's (MIT), announced on 30 June 2026 and tagged 0.1.0 days later, so it's young and its author calls it alpha. It grew out of a practical problem: at his studio, working on an Unreal Engine 5 game, they plugged in Dirk Gregorius's Rubikon-Lite (derived from Valve's Rubikon, the engine behind *Half-Life: Alyx*), and Catto then replaced most of it with code from Box2D v3. It's a C17 library with Box2D's API style and its "soft step" solver, and its announcement lists early users including Facepunch's s&box.

**Bullet** is Erwin Coumans's (zlib licence), started in the mid-2000s while he worked at Sony and continued at AMD. For years it was *the* open-source 3D engine: it's behind physics plugins for Blender, Maya, Houdini and Cinema 4D, and Godot 3.0 made it its default 3D engine in 2018. Godot 4 then dropped it for its own engine. Its last tagged release is 3.25 (September 2022). The repository still sees occasional commits, but its README now points newcomers to **PyBullet**, its Python bindings for robotics and machine learning, which is where most of its use is today.

All three step at 60 Hz with their default settings: Jolt with one collision step and 10 velocity iterations, Box3D with 4 substeps, Bullet with one step of 10 solver iterations. All three start the **Pyramid** (78 boxes) at rest and hold it. Jolt's is asleep after 0.50 s, Box3D's after 0.72 s and Bullet's after 2.03 s, because Bullet only puts a body to sleep after 2 seconds below its thresholds (0.8 m/s and 1 rad/s by default), where Jolt and Box3D wait half a second.

## The tall stack

Switch to **Tower of 20**: twenty cubes, every other one turned 45°. Box3D holds it with its defaults, and so does Bullet. Jolt, with its default 10 velocity steps, slowly lets it lean and topple; with 30 velocity steps it stands and is asleep after 0.50 s. Set Box3D to **1** substep and its tower falls too.

That's the [solver lesson](/physics/solving-contacts/) in three real engines. All three are sequential-impulse solvers with warm starting, and they differ in how they get rid of overlap:

- **Jolt** solves velocities with a fixed number of iterations, then runs 2 position iterations that correct overlap directly (scaled by a Baumgarte factor of 0.2).
- **Bullet** uses **split impulse** by default for deep overlap (more than 4 cm): that overlap is fixed with a separate "push" velocity that moves bodies apart in the position update and is then thrown away, so it never becomes real momentum, the same idea as a position pass done inside the velocity solver. Shallower overlap, including ordinary resting contact, is corrected Baumgarte-style inside the normal velocity solve.
- **Box3D**'s soft step spends its effort on substeps, each of which also moves the bodies, with soft contacts and a relax pass.

None of the defaults is wrong: a 20-high stack of unsupported cubes is rare in a game, and Jolt's defaults favour a lower cost per step. If you need tall stacks in Jolt, raise `mNumVelocitySteps`, or add collision steps.

## Fast bodies

Switch to **Fast ball**: a 30 cm steel ball at 600 m/s towards a 10 cm plate, starting where no step lands it inside the plate. Box3D stops it with its defaults, and loses it if you untick **Continuous collision**. Jolt and Bullet both lose it by default, and stop it with their continuous options ticked.

That's the [sleeping and fast bodies lesson](/physics/sleeping-and-fast-bodies/) again, as three different defaults:

- **Box3D** sweeps any dynamic body that moves far enough in a step to risk tunnelling (by default, more than a quarter of its thinnest width) against static geometry, unless you turn continuous collision off for the whole world. Marking a body as a *bullet* also sweeps it against moving bodies.
- **Jolt** sets a **motion quality** per body: `Discrete` by default, or `LinearCast`, which sweeps the body's shape along its motion and moves it to the time of impact.
- **Bullet**'s continuous collision is opt-in per body: give it a **motion threshold** (sweep when it moves further than this in a step) and a **swept-sphere radius** (sweep a sphere this size, inside the shape). It's a conservative sphere sweep rather than an exact shape cast, which is why the radius has to fit inside the body.

Jolt's and Box3D's speculative contacts don't help here: both only create a contact once shapes are within a fixed couple of centimetres (a margin sized for resting stability), which a ball moving metres per step jumps straight past. Without their continuous options, all three lose this ball even at 30 m/s, where it moves half a metre a step.

## Handles

Jolt and Box3D hand out ids, not pointers, exactly like the [generational handles](/engine/handles-and-allocators/) in the engine section. Bullet, older, hands out pointers:

- A Jolt **`BodyID`** is 32 bits: a 23-bit index, an 8-bit sequence number that changes when a slot is reused, and one bit the broadphase uses internally.
- A Box3D **`b3BodyId`** is a small struct: a 32-bit index (stored plus one, so a zeroed id is null), a 16-bit world index and a 16-bit generation. Every Box3D object has one, and using a stale one asserts in debug builds.
- A Bullet body is a **`btRigidBody*`** you allocate yourself, along with its shape and motion state, and delete yourself after removing it from the world. A stale pointer is a crash, not a check.

```cpp
// Jolt: settings object, then the body interface.
BodyCreationSettings settings(new BoxShape(Vec3(0.5f, 0.5f, 0.5f)), RVec3(0, 10, 0), Quat::sIdentity(),
                              EMotionType::Dynamic, Layers::MOVING);
BodyID id = physics_system.GetBodyInterface().CreateAndAddBody(settings, EActivation::Activate);

// Box3D: a def struct for the body, another for the shape.
b3BodyDef body_def = b3DefaultBodyDef();
body_def.type = b3_dynamicBody;
body_def.position = { 0, 10, 0 };
b3BodyId body = b3CreateBody(world, &body_def);
b3BoxHull box = b3MakeBoxHull(0.5f, 0.5f, 0.5f);
b3ShapeDef shape_def = b3DefaultShapeDef();
b3CreateHullShape(body, &shape_def, &box.base);

// Bullet: you own the shape, the motion state and the body.
btBoxShape* shape = new btBoxShape(btVector3(0.5f, 0.5f, 0.5f));
btVector3 inertia;
shape->calculateLocalInertia(1000.0f, inertia);
btTransform start(btQuaternion::getIdentity(), btVector3(0, 10, 0));
btRigidBody* rb = new btRigidBody(1000.0f, new btDefaultMotionState(start), shape, inertia);
dynamics_world->addRigidBody(rb);
```

Jolt's `BodyInterface` comes in two flavours: the default one takes a lock (from an array of mutexes shared between bodies), and `GetBodyInterfaceNoLock()` doesn't, for code that knows nothing else is touching those bodies. The [flecs lesson](/physics/physics-in-flecs/) builds on ids like these, and on why pointers are the wrong thing to keep in a component.

## The step and threads

```cpp
// Jolt: you supply a temp allocator and a job system.
physics_system.Update(1.0f / 60.0f, 1 /* collision steps */, &temp_allocator, &job_system);

// Box3D: the world was created with task callbacks (or none, for single-threaded).
b3World_Step(world, 1.0f / 60.0f, 4 /* substeps */);

// Bullet: pass the frame time; it runs as many fixed steps as fit (up to 10 here).
dynamics_world->stepSimulation(frame_seconds, 10, 1.0f / 60.0f);
```

- **Jolt** builds each step as a graph of jobs and runs it on a `JobSystem` you provide: subclass it to use your engine's scheduler, or use the bundled `JobSystemThreadPool`. Collision detection, then one island per job in the velocity solver, with very large islands split into parallel batches.
- **Box3D** asks for two callbacks in its world definition, `enqueueTask` and `finishTask`, and calls them to spread work over your workers; there's an optional built-in scheduler. Inside the solver it **colours the constraint graph** so that constraints in one colour share no bodies and can be solved in parallel and in SIMD batches.
- **Bullet**'s `stepSimulation` has the [fixed-step loop](/physics/the-step/) built in: give it the frame's time, a maximum number of steps and the fixed step, and it accumulates, steps, and by default hands your motion states a transform a step behind, interpolated from each body's velocity. The core library is single-threaded; multithreaded solvers came later as optional extras.

All three run single-threaded on this page.

## Determinism and replay

- **Jolt** is deterministic on one platform if you make the same API calls in the same order. Cross-platform determinism is a build option, `CROSS_PLATFORM_DETERMINISTIC`, off by default and about 8% slower. `SaveState` and `RestoreState` snapshot what the step changes, which is enough for rollback networking as long as the game handles bodies added or removed between snapshots.
- **Box3D** lists cross-platform determinism as a feature, along with **recording and replay**: record every API call into a buffer, then play it back frame by frame or seek to a frame, and validate that a replay reproduces the recording. It doesn't offer rollback determinism yet, though: there's no public way to restore a past world state and resume with identical results (the snapshot that seeds a recording is internal for now; Box2D 3.2 made its own public, as an experimental API). Its determinism suits lockstep networking and reproducing bugs.
- **Bullet** makes no cross-platform promise. It's repeatable on one machine for the same calls in the same order, which is what most tools and robotics uses needed.

## Characters

The [characters lesson](/physics/queries-and-characters/) covers this in detail. **Jolt** ships two finished controllers, `CharacterVirtual` (a mover that walks stairs and sticks to floors) and `Character` (a rigid body). **Box3D** ships a mover *toolkit*: a capsule cast, a plane collector and a plane solver, with stepping, jumping and everything else up to you. **Bullet** has `btKinematicCharacterController`, a kinematic capsule with a step height and a maximum slope; it's simple, and many projects that used Bullet wrote their own.

## Side by side

| | Jolt | Box3D | Bullet |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Language, licence** | C++, MIT | C17, MIT | C++, zlib |
| **Maturity** | 5.6; shipping in large games | 0.1.0, alpha (June 2026) | 3.25 (2022); now mostly PyBullet |
| **Handles** | 32-bit `BodyID` | `b3BodyId` struct | raw pointers |
| **Solver** | sequential impulses: 10 velocity + 2 position iterations | soft step: 4 substeps with relax | sequential impulses: 10 iterations, split impulse |
| **Fast bodies** | per body: `Discrete` or `LinearCast` | sweep against static for fast bodies; bullets against everything | per body: swept sphere above a motion threshold |
| **Threading** | job graph on your `JobSystem` | task callbacks; graph colouring and SIMD | single-threaded core; optional parallel solvers |
| **Determinism** | same platform; cross-platform as a build option; state save/restore | cross-platform; recording and replay | same machine |
| **Characters** | `CharacterVirtual` and `Character` | mover toolkit | `btKinematicCharacterController` |
| **Also** | vehicles, soft bodies, ragdolls, double precision | double-precision positions | soft bodies, Featherstone articulated bodies, vehicles, robotics through PyBullet |

## Takeaways

- **Jolt**: mature, broad, shipping now, C++; tune iterations and collision steps for your content.
- **Box3D**: new, compact C API, Box2D v3's soft-step solver, determinism and replay built in.
- **Bullet**: the long-time open-source standard, with the fixed step built into its world; today mostly robotics, through PyBullet.
- Jolt and Box3D hand out **generational ids**; Bullet hands out **pointers**.
- Their **defaults differ** in ways that matter: stack stiffness, continuous collision, sleep timing, characters.

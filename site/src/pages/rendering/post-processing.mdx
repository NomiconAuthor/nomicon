The lighting lessons compute **radiance**: how much light leaves each surface towards the camera. Nothing limits it to 1. A lamp is hundreds of times brighter than the wall it lights, and a display can show a range of maybe a few hundred to one, in 8 bits per channel. Between the lit frame and the screen sits a chain of full-screen passes that decide how that light becomes pixels, and that clean up what rasterising one sample per pixel leaves behind. This lesson builds the usual chain on your GPU, one pass at a time.

## The frame

<RenderWidget kind="post" />

The courtyard at night: sixteen point lights, each with a small cube that glows at 24 times the brightness of white, and a crate sliding from side to side. Every frame:

1. **scene (HDR)** draws it into a half-float (`rgba16float`) target, so values above 1 survive;
2. **TAA resolve** blends it into a history, a running average of previous frames;
3. **bloom** blurs the bright parts through a chain of 5 downsamples and 4 upsamples;
4. **tone map** mixes the bloom in, applies exposure and a tone curve, encodes sRGB and writes the canvas.

That's 12 passes with everything on, each a full-screen triangle after the first. **Split** shows the frame as drawn, exposed and clamped with no post-processing, on the left half. The list beside the frame shows the passes in order and their sizes; it follows your settings.

## HDR targets

An 8-bit target clips at 1: a lamp and a white wall both store 1.0, and nothing later can tell them apart. A half-float target stores up to 65,504 with about three significant digits, at 8 bytes per pixel instead of 4: 15.8 MB at 1920 × 1080. Engines also use `R11G11B10` floats, 4 bytes with no alpha and less precision; WebGPU offers it as a render target only with the optional `rg11b10ufloat-renderable` feature, and WebGL2 can render to float targets only with `EXT_color_buffer_float`. If the widget's WebGL2 can't, it says so and uses 8 bits, and the lamps clip.

## Exposure and tone mapping

**Exposure** scales the scene, like a camera's: +1 EV doubles every value. Games set it by hand per area or measure it each frame (auto-exposure, from the average log luminance, which is how Reinhard and his co-authors set the "key" of an image). Then a **tone curve** squeezes the exposed values into 0..1.

- **None** clamps at 1. Everything bright becomes the same flat white, and colours shift: a bright orange light clamps its red channel first and turns yellow.
- **Reinhard**: *x* / (1 + *x*), from Reinhard, Stark, Shirley and Ferwerda's [*Photographic Tone Reproduction for Digital Images*](https://www-old.cs.utah.edu/docs/techreports/2002/pdf/UUCS-02-001.pdf) (2002). It never clips, but it's slow to get there: 1 becomes 0.5, and it takes an input of 99 to reach 0.99, so highlights look grey.
- **Reinhard with a white point** *W*: *x* (1 + *x* / *W*²) / (1 + *x*), which reaches exactly 1 at *W*. With *W* = 4, 1 becomes 0.53.
- **ACES fit**: Krzysztof Narkowicz's [fit](https://knarkowicz.wordpress.com/2016/01/06/aces-filmic-tone-mapping-curve/) of the Academy's filmic curve, five numbers in a rational function. A **filmic** curve has a toe that deepens shadows and a shoulder that rolls highlights off; 1 becomes 0.80 (Narkowicz: "1 on input maps to ~0.8 on output"; multiply the input by 0.6 to match the original curve), and 0.99 is reached at an input of 5.55.

The plot beside the frame shows all four, and the one in use. Each of them works on red, green and blue separately, so saturated bright colours shift hue and saturate as each channel hits the shoulder at a different time. **AgX**, which replaced Filmic as Blender's default view transform in Blender 4.0, is built to avoid that: in Blender's words, "bright colors go towards white, similar to real cameras". It isn't in the widget; it needs a colour-space transform around the curve, not just a curve.

## Bloom

Real lenses and eyes scatter a little of the light from bright sources into the surrounding image. **Bloom** imitates that: blur the bright parts of the frame and add them back. A blur wide enough to matter is expensive at full resolution, so it's done on a chain of half-size images. The widget follows Jorge Jimenez's [*Next Generation Post Processing in Call of Duty: Advanced Warfare*](https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2014/index.html) (SIGGRAPH 2014), as LearnOpenGL's [*Physically Based Bloom*](https://learnopengl.com/Guest-Articles/2022/Phys.-Based-Bloom) describes it:

- **Downsample** five times, each with a 13-tap filter: five overlapping 2 × 2 boxes, weighted, which avoids the shimmering a plain 2 × 2 average gives as things move. At 1920 × 1080 the levels are 960 × 540, 480 × 270, 240 × 135, 120 × 67 and 60 × 33.
- **Upsample** back up with a 3 × 3 tent filter, adding each level to the next larger one, so the result is a sum of blurs of increasing width, close to a wide Gaussian.
- **Mix** it into the HDR frame with a small weight, 0.04 here as in Jimenez's talk, before tone mapping. There's no threshold: everything blooms a little, but only very bright pixels bloom visibly.

At 1920 × 1080 the chain holds 10.5 MB of half-float textures. Jimenez also weights the first downsample by 1 / (1 + luminance) in groups of four ("Karis average") to stop single very bright pixels flickering; the widget doesn't.

**Order matters.** Choose **After**: the chain then blurs the tone-mapped image, where the lamps are already squeezed to at most 1, so the glow around them almost disappears. Bloom belongs before tone mapping, where a lamp still has 24 times the energy of a white wall to spread around.

## Temporal anti-aliasing

One sample per pixel aliases: edges stair-step, and thin things flicker as they move. MSAA helps edges only, and doesn't work well with deferred shading. **Temporal anti-aliasing** spreads the samples over time instead:

- **Jitter**: each frame, the projection is shifted by a different sub-pixel offset, so each pixel samples a different point inside itself. The widget uses the first 8 points of the Halton (2, 3) sequence, a low-discrepancy sequence that covers the pixel evenly, and repeats.
- **History**: the resolve blends the new frame into an accumulated history: history × (1 − α) + new × α. With α = 0.1 (Salvi: "Typically we blend 10% of the current frame"), the result has about the noise of an average of 19 frames.
- **Reprojection**: things move, so the history must be read where the surface was last frame. The resolve rebuilds each pixel's position from depth and projects it with last frame's camera; engines also write **motion vectors** for moving objects. This widget's camera doesn't move and its crate has no motion vectors, on purpose.
- **History rejection**: when the surface behind a pixel changes (something moved, or was uncovered), the history is wrong. The usual fix, which Brian Karis's [*High Quality Temporal Supersampling*](https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2014/index.html) (SIGGRAPH 2014) made widely known, is to **clamp** the history colour to the range of colours in the new frame's 3 × 3 neighbourhood: a colour that isn't around any more can't survive.

Untick **Clamp history** and watch the crate: it smears a trail. That's **ghosting**: without rejection, a colour that's gone still makes up (1 − α)<sup>*n*</sup> of the pixel *n* frames later, 35% after 10 frames, and it takes 22 frames to fall below 10%. Clamping removes most of the trail, at a price: it also rejects legitimate history on thin, high-contrast details, so they flicker or soften. Real TAA spends most of its code on this trade-off: clipping towards the average rather than clamping, variance-based bounds ([Salvi, 2016](https://developer.download.nvidia.com/gameworks/events/GDC2016/msalvi_temporal_supersampling.pdf)), motion-vector checks, and sharpening afterwards.

**Blend in: Tone-mapped** divides each colour by 1 + its luminance before blending and undoes it after. In linear space, one very bright sample, such as a lamp's edge, dominates the average and flickers as the jitter moves; in the compressed space it weighs about as much as any other.

## Upscaling

Choose a **render scale** of 75% or 50%: the scene is drawn at that fraction of the width and height, a quarter of the pixels at 50%, and the TAA resolve writes a full-size history from it. Because each frame samples different sub-pixel positions, the history gathers more detail than any single low-resolution frame holds. That's the idea behind temporal upscalers: Unreal's TSR, AMD's FSR 2 and later, Intel's XeSS and NVIDIA's DLSS all accumulate jittered low-resolution frames into a full-resolution history, the last two using a neural network to decide how much of the history to trust. Turn TAA off at 50% and the frame is just stretched.

## The order of the passes

A typical engine's chain, after lighting: **TAA or upscaling** first, on the HDR scene, so that everything after it sees a stable, full-resolution image; then effects that model the camera (depth of field, motion blur, **bloom**, lens effects), still in HDR; then **exposure and tone mapping**, then colour grading, usually as a lookup table, and the encoding for the display; then film grain, sharpening and the UI, which is drawn last and never tone-mapped. The widget's two order switches show the two mistakes that are easiest to see: bloom after tone mapping loses the energy of bright lights, and TAA in linear space lets single bright samples flicker.

<Tip title="HDR displays">
On an HDR display the tone curve's target changes: the output is no longer 0..1 for an sRGB screen but absolute brightness up to the display's peak, in a wider gamut, encoded with PQ (SMPTE ST 2084) or HLG. The chain is the same; the last pass, and the shoulder of the curve, change.
</Tip>

## Takeaways

- Light the scene in **HDR** (half floats: 8 bytes per pixel) so bright things stay bright until the end.
- **Exposure** then a **tone curve** map it to the display: Reinhard never clips but greys highlights; filmic curves (the ACES fit maps 1 to 0.80) add a toe and a shoulder; AgX avoids per-channel hue shifts.
- **Bloom**: a chain of 13-tap downsamples and tent upsamples, mixed in at about 0.04, before tone mapping.
- **TAA**: jitter the projection, reproject and blend into a history (α = 0.1 is about 19 frames), and **clamp** the history to the current neighbourhood to stop **ghosting**. The same machinery upscales.
